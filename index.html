Why FedRAMP introduced Tailored

An agency considering an online tool had two questions. What harm could a loss of confidentiality, integrity, or availability cause? And what information would staff actually put in it? A Low impact rating answered the first. Eligibility for Li-SaaS also depended on the second.

The 2017 initiative gave agencies a narrower path for fully operational SaaS offerings. The service could hold the minimum personal information needed for login. A tool collecting personal details in a survey, for example, fell outside that boundary even if the agency categorized the system as Low. For a qualifying service, the agency could focus testing on specified controls and accept the provider’s attestation for others. [1]

Reading the Rev. 5 workbook

What counted as evidence?

The Low and Li-SaaS worksheets each list 156 control and enhancement rows. The Li-SaaS worksheet assigns a tailoring action to each row. That action tells a reviewer what evidence to look for. [6]

For the 75 attestation rows, the provider affirmed the safeguard existed. For the 45 assessment rows, an independent assessor examined the implementation. For the 20 conditional rows, the provider either documented the control for assessment or attested that the triggering condition was absent. Of the remaining 16, FedRAMP judged 11 outside the security needs of this SaaS use, assigned three to the federal government, and split two by use case. [6]

Two controls in the same family

AC-1 · Policy and procedures. Low included the access control policy in its assessment scope. Li-SaaS allowed the provider to attest that it had the policy. The assessor did not need to test that row separately.

AC-2 · Account management. Both baselines called for assessment. Under Li-SaaS the provider still had to explain how it managed accounts and the assessor had to examine that implementation. Signing the policy attestation for AC-1 supplied no evidence about whether departing users’ accounts were disabled. [6]
